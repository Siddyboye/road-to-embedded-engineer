## Why this matters

Firmware engineers who cannot reason about one resistor destroy pins, blame software for hardware faults, and cannot read the schematic they are given. Three laws cover most of what you will ever need.

## Prerequisites, hardware, time

An LED, a few resistors (220 Ω, 330 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ), a breadboard, any ESP32 board for its 3.3 V pin, and a multimeter if you have one. 60 minutes.

## How it works

**Voltage** is the push, measured between two points. **Current** is the flow, measured through one point. **Resistance** is how hard a part resists the flow.

- **Ohm’s law:** V = I × R. One resistor, three quantities; know two and you have the third.
- **Kirchhoff’s voltage law:** around any loop, the drops add up to the supply. That is why the resistor gets “whatever the LED leaves”.
- **Kirchhoff’s current law:** what flows into a junction flows out. In a single loop the current is the same everywhere, which is what the dots show.
- **Power:** P = V × I. It becomes heat, and it comes out of your battery.

An LED is not a resistor. Below its forward voltage it barely conducts; above it, the current would rise without limit. The resistor is what sets the current, so an LED straight across a supply is a short circuit with a brief light show.

The same reasoning sizes every pin you will ever use:

```
R = (V_supply − V_forward) / I_wanted
  = (3.3 V − 1.9 V) / 0.005 A
  = 280 Ω  →  use 330 Ω, the next standard value up
```

## Go deeper

A GPIO pin is a pair of transistor switches: one to the supply, one to ground. Each has some resistance of its own, so the pin’s voltage sags as you draw more current. The datasheet’s “DC characteristics” table states how much current a pin may source and sink, and what voltage it guarantees while doing so. Find that table for the ESP32-S3 and write down two numbers: the maximum current per pin and the output-high voltage at that current. You will need them in lesson 0.6.

- [ ] With the LED circuit on a GPIO-level supply, compute the power in the resistor at 330 Ω. Is a standard quarter-watt resistor in any danger?
- [ ] Two 1 kΩ resistors in series, then in parallel: predict the total, then measure it.

## When to use it, when not to

| Load | Drive it from a pin? |
| --- | --- |
| Indicator LED at a few milliamps | Yes, with a resistor |
| Several LEDs, a buzzer, a relay coil, a motor | No. Use a transistor or a driver; the pin only switches the transistor |
| Another chip’s logic input | Yes: inputs draw almost nothing |

## Common mistakes and interview angles

- **LED without a resistor.** Works for a while, then the pin or the LED fails.
- **Measuring current across a part.** A multimeter in current mode is nearly a short circuit. It goes in series, in the loop.
- **Interview angle.** “Size a resistor for an LED on a 3.3 V GPIO” is asked to see whether you subtract the forward voltage and mention the pin’s current limit.
