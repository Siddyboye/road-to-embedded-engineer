## Why this matters

Every sensor reading, every audio sample and every input to a neural network in Phase 9 is a continuous signal turned into numbers. Do it too slowly and the numbers describe a signal that never existed, with nothing in the data to warn you.

## Prerequisites, hardware, time

An S3 board and one jumper wire. Lessons 0.5 and 0.7. 75 minutes.

```c
adc_oneshot_unit_init_cfg_t unit_cfg = { .unit_id = unit };

adc_oneshot_chan_cfg_t chan_cfg = { .bitwidth = ADC_BITWIDTH_DEFAULT, .atten = ADC_ATTEN_DB_12 };

/* ... once per sample period ... */
adc_oneshot_read(adc, channel, &raw);
```

At 2000 samples per second the square wave is obvious; values vary a little:

```expected
4095
4095
4095
0
0
0
```

At exactly 100 samples per second, every sample lands on the same point of the wave and the output is a constant. A 100 Hz signal has become DC.

## How it works

**Sampling** reads the signal at regular instants and discards everything between them.

**The Nyquist limit.** To recover a frequency you need more than two samples per cycle. So a sample rate *fs* can represent frequencies up to *fs*/2 and nothing above.

**Aliasing.** A frequency above *fs*/2 does not vanish. It folds back and appears as a lower one: a tone at *f* sampled at *fs* shows up at the distance between *f* and the nearest multiple of *fs*. The lab’s dashed line passes through every sample exactly, which is the uncomfortable part: no amount of software can tell the two apart afterwards. The only defence is a filter *before* the converter that removes what you cannot represent.

See the same thing in the frequency domain. Push a tone above half the sample rate and watch where its peak lands:

::widget fft

**Quantization.** The converter also rounds each sample to one of 2^bits levels. A 12-bit ADC over a 3.3 V range has steps of about 0.8 mV. Drag the resolution down in the sampling lab to see the staircase. The rounding error behaves like added noise, about half a step in size.

**Resolution is not accuracy.** Twelve bits of resolution with 20 mV of noise on the supply is not twelve useful bits. Averaging several samples trades speed for a quieter number; calibration fixes offset and gain. Lesson 2.6 measures both on your board.

## When to use it, when not to

| Signal | Sample at | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Room temperature | Once a second or slower | It cannot change faster |
| Accelerometer for gestures | 50 to 200 Hz | Human motion is mostly below 20 Hz |
| Vibration of a motor | Several kHz | Faults show up as high harmonics |
| Speech | 16 kHz | Intelligibility needs up to about 7 kHz |

Sample as slowly as the signal allows: every extra sample costs power, memory and processing.

## Common mistakes and interview angles

- **Sampling “fast enough” by feel.** Compute the highest frequency present, including noise.
- **Trusting the last bits of an ADC reading.** Look at the spread of repeated readings first.
- **Interview angle.** “What is aliasing and how do you prevent it?” The complete answer includes the analog filter before the ADC, not only the sample rate.
